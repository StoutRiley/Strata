Goal Completion Tips

SMART goals

SMART is a checklist for writing clear goals. A goal that meets all five criteria is easier to plan, track, and finish.

  • Specific: The goal states exactly what will be achieved.
  • Measurable: Progress and completion can be judged by a number or a clear yes or no.
  • Achievable: The goal is challenging but realistic given the time, skills, and resources available.
  • Relevant: The goal supports a larger purpose that matters.
  • Time-bound: A target date is set, and each smaller step is finished early enough to meet it.
Example: "Get fit" becomes "Walk 30 minutes, four days a week, for eight weeks."

Introduced by George T. Doran in 1981.

OKRs (Objectives and Key Results)

OKRs are a goal-setting framework that pairs an inspiring objective with a few measurable key results. Together they show where a person or team is headed and how to tell whether they arrived.

  • Objective: A qualitative, motivating statement of what will be achieved. It answers the question "Where do we want to go?"
  • Key results: Typically three to five quantifiable outcomes that show whether the objective was achieved. They measure results, not tasks or activity.
  • Cycle and scoring: OKRs usually run in short cycles, often a quarter. Each key result is scored at the end, commonly from 0.0 to 1.0, and a score near 0.7 is often seen as strong for an ambitious target.
Example: Objective: "Open a popular neighborhood bakery." Key results: "Serve 200 customers per week" and "Reach a 4.5-star average review."

Developed at Intel by Andy Grove and popularized at Google by John Doerr, who described the method in Measure What Matters (2018).

Planning the steps

A large goal feels distant when the path to it is unclear. Planning the steps replaces that uncertainty with a clear sequence of actions between the starting point and the goal.

  • Work backward: Begin with the finished goal and ask what must be true just before it, then before that, until the chain reaches today.
  • Keep steps small and checkable: Each step is small enough to finish and clear enough to know when it is finished.
  • Detail the next several steps: Plan the next three to five steps carefully and leave later steps rough until they come closer. Knowing what comes next removes hesitation.
  • Revisit the plan: Add, reorder, or remove steps as new information appears.
Example: A writer with a book in mind lists the steps: outline the chapters, draft chapter one, finish the first draft, revise, and submit. The detailed plan covers only the first three.

Draws on work breakdown structures from project management and the "next action" habit from David Allen's Getting Things Done.

Owning the gap

Owning the gap means seeing clearly where things stand today, deciding clearly where they should be, and accepting responsibility for the distance between the two.

  • Current position: Describe the starting point honestly, using facts rather than excuses.
  • Desired position: Define the target clearly enough to recognize it when it is reached.
  • Ownership: Accept that closing the gap is up to you. Focus on what is within your control and act on it.
Example: A student aims for an A in a course but currently averages a C+. Owning the gap means naming the C+ plainly, naming the A as the target, and choosing the study habits that will close the distance.

Related to the idea of creative tension described by Peter Senge in The Fifth Discipline (1990).

Growth mindset

A growth mindset is the belief that abilities and results can improve through effort, good strategies, and feedback. It contrasts with a fixed mindset, which treats talent as unchangeable.

  • Setbacks are information: A failure shows what to change. It is not a verdict on ability.
  • Effort builds skill: Abilities grow through practice, so progress comes from steady work.
  • Feedback is useful: Seek it out and apply it instead of avoiding it.
  • Change is always possible: A new approach can be tried at any point on the way to a higher goal.
Example: A first-time baker's bread comes out dense. Rather than deciding they cannot bake, they check the recipe, adjust the rising time, and try again.

Popularized by psychologist Carol Dweck in her 2006 book Mindset.

Signal-to-noise ratio

The term comes from engineering and communications. The signal is the useful information, and the noise is everything that hides it. In goal setting, the signal is the small set of actions and measurements that move a goal forward. The noise is busywork, distractions, and numbers that look impressive but change nothing.

A higher ratio means more effort goes to what matters. The ratio improves by removing low-value work, tracking only a few meaningful measures, and doing the most important work first.

Example: A runner preparing for a race tracks weekly distance and finishing times. These are signal. The color of the shoes and the number of likes on a training photo are noise.

Borrowed from signal processing and communications engineering.

Putting the principles to use

  • SMART: Write each step so that someone else could tell whether it is finished.
  • OKRs: Pair a motivating objective with a few measurable results, and judge progress by outcomes rather than activity.
  • Planning the steps: Name the next several steps so there is never a question about what to do next.
  • Owning the gap: Describe the starting point honestly and the target clearly, then act on what is within your control.
  • Growth mindset: After a setback, ask what it taught and adjust the next step.
  • Signal to noise: Regularly remove tasks and measurements that do not move the goal forward.
